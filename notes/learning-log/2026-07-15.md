## 🔐 Security Fundamentals → [full note](../security-fundamentals.md)

**The core reframe:** every other part of engineering asks *"does it work?"* Security asks *"can it be made to do something it shouldn't?"* You're not designing for the average user — you're designing against a motivated adversary **who reads your source code**.

What actually stuck:

- **Security is risk management, not perfection** — same logic as an SLO. You can't be 100% secure; you reduce risk to an acceptable level. **Assume breach.**
- **The principles outlive the vulnerability lists:** defense in depth, least privilege, fail closed, secure by default, complete mediation (check authz on *every* request, not once at login), minimize attack surface, don't roll your own crypto, zero trust.
- **Fail closed vs. fail open is a real fork in the road.** My caching note says *fail open* (serve from origin if the cache dies). Security says **fail closed** (on error, deny). Knowing which kind of component you're building is the entire point — and getting it wrong is now OWASP **A10**.
- **STRIDE is structured paranoia** applied at design time: Spoofing/Tampering/Repudiation/Information disclosure/DoS/Elevation of privilege, each mapping to the property it violates. Best habit: **draw the trust boundaries** — that's where the bugs live.
- **AuthN ≠ AuthZ, and AuthZ is where things break.** Broken access control is #1 because apps authenticate you correctly then forget to check whether *this* user owns *that* record. Hiding a button is UI, not security.
- **The OWASP Top 10:2025 is different from the 2021 list I half-remembered** — worth relearning: A01 Broken Access Control, A02 Misconfiguration (↑), **A03 Software Supply Chain Failures (NEW)**, A04 Crypto, A05 Injection (↓), A06 Insecure Design, A07 Auth, A08 Integrity, A09 Logging & **Alerting**, **A10 Mishandling of Exceptional Conditions (NEW)**.
- **Encoding ≠ hashing ≠ encryption.** Base64 is an alphabet change, not security. Password hashing needs a **slow, salted KDF (argon2id)** — SHA-256 is *too fast* (GPUs do billions/sec). Use **authenticated** encryption (AES-GCM) or you get confidentiality without integrity.
- **Injection has one general fix:** never build interpreter strings by concatenation. Parameterize (SQL), array-exec (shell), never `eval`. Blocklists ("strip `<script>`") always lose — attackers have infinite encodings.
- **Committed a secret? Rotate it.** Deleting the commit doesn't help — history and forks persist.

> One-liner to remember: *validate input on an allowlist, encode output, authorize server-side on every request — and fail closed.*

## 🔗 Connections I noticed

- **Supply chain went from "dependency hygiene" to OWASP #3 — and my pnpm note is literally the mitigation.** pnpm v11 blocking lifecycle scripts by default (`allowBuilds`), gating on `minimumReleaseAge`, and shipping `pnpm sbom` are A03 controls. I'd read that note as "tooling trivia"; it's actually security engineering. **Two notes, same threat, opposite directions.**
- **A09 is my observability note.** "Security Logging & Alerting Failures" is a top-10 risk — you can't respond to what you can't see. Alerting on authn failures and authz denials is the same discipline as alerting on SLO burn.
- **Defense in depth is the layered evaluator again.** Cheap deterministic gates first, expensive deep checks behind — the exact shape of the agent evaluator I designed, and of caching hierarchies. This pattern is everywhere.
- **Fail closed / fail open is the sharpest contrast I've hit so far.** Availability components and security controls want *opposite* failure modes. Most of my notes have been "here's the trade-off dial"; this one is "here's which direction to turn it, and it depends on what you're building."
