## 🌐 CAP deepened → [full note § 2](../consistency-models-cap.md#2-the-cap-theorem)

What I added, and what actually stuck:

- **The proof is trivially reconstructible, and I should have learned it sooner.** Brewer *conjectured* CAP (2000); **Gilbert & Lynch proved it** (2002). Two replicas, a partition, a write to N1, a read from N2 — N2 either answers stale (not linearizable) or refuses (not available). That's the entire theorem. Everything else is interpretation.
- **Harvest & yield is the dial I actually want.** Fox & Brewer (1999): **yield** = fraction of requests completed, **harvest** = fraction of data reflected in the answer. So instead of binary up/down, you can **degrade gracefully** — serve a search over 90% of the index rather than failing. Binary CAP literally cannot express the thing you do in production.
- **Brewer retracted his own slogan.** "CAP Twelve Years Later" (2012): "pick 2 of 3" is misleading — partitions are *rare*, so don't forfeit C or A the rest of the time. The real framing is **explicit partition management**: detect → enter partition mode (limit ops, log intent) → recover **and compensate**. And the choice is **per-invariant, not global**.
- **CAP's "availability" is operationally meaningless.** Kleppmann's critique: it only requires a non-failed node *eventually* responds — **no latency bound**. A 100-year response is "available." CAP also says nothing about latency, which is what actually hurts daily. Real databases fit neither label cleanly.
- **You cannot detect a partition.** You infer one from a **timeout**, which cannot distinguish partitioned from slow from dead. So "enter partition mode" always fires on a *heuristic*: too short → false failovers, too long → prolonged outage.

> One-liner to remember: *CAP is excellent intuition and a terrible classification system — under a partition you must choose, but you can't tell when the partition started, and "available" in the theorem doesn't mean available.*

## 🔗 Connections I noticed

- **CAP recovery IS the saga pattern.** Brewer's step 3 — "restore consistency *and compensate* for what you did wrong in partition mode" — is exactly compensating transactions from my message-queue and durable-execution notes. The theory paper and the implementation pattern are the same idea, and I'd never connected them.
- **"You can't detect a partition" = FLP.** A timeout can't distinguish slow from dead — the identical impossibility from my consensus note. It's why **split-brain and failover tuning are hard** (multi-region note): the theory says "when partitioned, choose," but production has to *guess* the moment.
- **Harvest/yield = graceful degradation + load shedding**, formalised. My load-balancing note reached the same conclusion from the ops side; Fox & Brewer got there in 1999 from the theory side.
